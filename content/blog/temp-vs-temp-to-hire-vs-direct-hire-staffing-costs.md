## How do temp, temp-to-hire, and direct hire compare?

The three options differ in who employs the worker, how you pay, and how much commitment you make up front.

| | Temporary | Temp-to-hire | Direct hire |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who employs the worker | The staffing agency | The agency during the trial, then you | You, from day one |
| How you pay | Hourly bill rate | Hourly bill rate, then the terms agreed for conversion | One-time placement fee |
| Who runs payroll, taxes, and workers' comp | The agency | The agency during the trial | You |
| How fast someone can start | Fastest. Often within days | Fast. Same as temporary | Slower. Interviews and notice periods |
| Commitment | None beyond the assignment | None until you decide to hire | A permanent hire |
| If the worker does not work out | The agency replaces them | The agency replaces them during the trial | Depends on the agency's guarantee |
| Best for | Peaks, projects, cover for absences | Ongoing roles where fit is hard to judge from an interview | Permanent and skilled roles with clear requirements |

## How does staffing agency pricing work?

### Temporary and temp-to-hire: the bill rate

For temporary and temp-to-hire workers, you pay the agency an hourly bill rate for every hour worked. The bill rate is the worker's hourly pay plus a markup.

The markup is often misread as pure agency profit. Most of it covers costs you would carry yourself if the worker were on your payroll:

- **Employer payroll taxes.** The employer pays 7.65% of wages for Social Security and Medicare, plus federal and state unemployment taxes.
- **Workers' compensation insurance.** This costs more for physical work such as warehouse, forklift, and production roles than for office work.
- **Recruiting and screening.** This covers advertising, interviewing, background checks, drug screening, and verifying certifications.
- **Payroll and administration.** This covers timekeeping, paychecks, tax filing, and employment compliance.
- **Support and replacement.** This covers check-ins after placement, and finding a replacement when a worker does not work out.
- **The agency's margin.**

### Direct hire: the placement fee

For direct hire, the agency recruits and screens candidates, and you hire the person onto your own payroll. You pay a one-time fee, usually calculated from the role's first-year salary. Many agencies offer a guarantee period: if the hire leaves early, they replace the person or refund part of the fee. Ask what the guarantee covers before you sign.

## What makes the cost go up or down?

The same agency can quote very different markups for different roles. The main drivers are:

- **Pay rate and skill level.** Roles that are harder to fill, such as IPC-certified electronics assemblers or experienced reach-truck operators, take more recruiting effort.
- **Shift.** Overnight and weekend roles have smaller candidate pools and higher turnover.
- **Volume and length.** A steady order for 20 workers over six months costs less per worker to support than a one-off order for a single day.
- **Workers' compensation risk.** Warehouse and industrial roles carry higher insurance costs than clerical roles.
- **Screening requirements.** Extra checks, such as a more detailed background check or a specific certification, add time and cost.

## Which option is right for you?

### Choose temporary staffing when:
- demand rises and falls, such as fall and holiday peak, a new contract, or a project with an end date
- you need to cover absences or leave
- you need people within days and can't run a full hiring process

### Choose temp-to-hire when:
- the role is ongoing, but attendance and fit are hard to judge from an interview
- you have had early turnover in the role before and want to see someone work first
- you want to keep the strongest workers from a peak season

### Choose direct hire when:
- the role is permanent and the skills can be checked up front, such as engineers, supervisors, and quality managers
- strong candidates won't leave a current job for a temporary start
- you want the person on your own benefits and payroll from day one

### Who temporary staffing is not right for
Temporary staffing is a poor fit for roles that need months of training before someone becomes productive, unless you plan to convert the worker. It's also a poor fit when your site isn't ready to give temporary workers the same safety training and supervision as your own staff. OSHA expects the host employer to provide site-specific training.

## What mistakes do employers make when comparing staffing costs?

- **Comparing bill rates alone.** A lower rate is no saving if the worker doesn't show up. One no-show on a production line or dock can cost more in lost output than a month of rate difference. Compare the cost per productive hour.
- **Not agreeing temp-to-hire terms up front.** Agree on the trial length and any conversion fee before the first day, not when you want to hire.
- **Leaving safety responsibilities unclear.** For forklift and other equipment roles, agree who covers training and the on-site evaluation before placement.
- **Ordering for day shift when the gap is nights.** Order for the schedule you actually need covered. A worker who wanted days won't stay on nights.

## What should you have ready before you call an agency?

To get an accurate quote and a fast start, have these ready:

1. The job title, duties, and any certifications required.
2. The number of workers and the start date.
3. Shift times, including overtime and weekend expectations.
4. The pay rate you have in mind, or ask the agency for a market rate.
5. Site details: location, safety equipment, dress code, and parking or transit access.
6. Whether you want temporary, temp-to-hire, or direct hire. If you aren't sure, ask the agency to recommend one.
